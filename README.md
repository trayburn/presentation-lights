# presentation-lights

Materials for the talk **"Turn On the Lights: The Real Future of Software Development"** by Tim Rayburn.

## The Talk

Every developer knows the fear: some random Tuesday, someone will launch "Insert Idea, Get Software" The Tool™ — and that will be it. You can't explain the technical path from here to there. It's an existential dread fueled by two years of relentless hype from an AI Media Machine that has a financial interest in your anxiety.

The problem isn't that the fear is irrational. The problem is that it's *vague* — and vague fear produces paralysis, not strategy. You can't defend against a monster you've never seen clearly.

This talk turns on the lights and shows you the actual shape of the thing under your bed. Three lights switch on:

1. **The Hidden Dependency** — AI is genuinely strong at writing code. But software exists to serve business domains full of context, history, and judgment that AI simply doesn't have. Somebody has to translate the idea into a specification precise enough to produce the *right* software, not just *plausible* software. That work requires domain expertise, human adaptability, and knowledge that compounds with experience.
2. **Compression, Not Extinction** — the historical record of automation is, overwhelmingly, a record of compression rather than replacement. ATMs increased bank teller employment. VisiCalc eliminated accounting clerks and created 200,000 more accountants. The right frame isn't "will AI take my job?" — it's "what happens to the freed capacity?" That question has a strategy. The first one doesn't.
3. **The Broken Ladder** — the real crisis isn't that seniors get replaced. It's that the organic learning path that always developed junior developers has been automated away. The profession doesn't have a talent problem. It has a development model problem. And that's a problem we know how to solve.

The talk closes with the six 201-level applied judgment skills — the "missing middle" of AI training that has nothing to do with prompting, transfers across every tool, and survives every model upgrade:

1. Context Assembly
2. Quality Judgment
3. Task Decomposition
4. Iterative Refinement
5. Workflow Integration
6. Frontier Recognition

The monster can write the code. The monster cannot understand the business, develop the next generation of engineers, or evaluate its own work without a human who knows what good looks like.

> *"Get in the car."*
